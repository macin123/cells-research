Project Description
In July 2023, two Florida statutes reshaped landlord-tenant relations across the state. CS/HB 1417 overrode local ordinances that had (i) prohibited landlords from refusing tenants who pay with federal Housing Choice Vouchers and (ii) required landlords to give sixty days' notice before raising rent. CS/HB 133 simultaneously allowed landlords statewide to offer a monthly fee or insurance policy in place of the traditional lump-sum security deposit. Together these laws changed housing access for roughly one hundred thousand voucher-holding Florida households, altered the move-in cost barrier facing every new renter, and reshaped rent-setting practices. This project tries to rigorous evaluate and quantify how landlords and tenants actually responded.
Undergraduate Research Assistants will play a critical role by manually validating the machine-learning classifier that identifies landlord refusal and deposit-alternative language in listings, assembling supplementary policy timelines across Florida jurisdictions, and contributing to the final report. Students will gain hands-on experience with large-scale housing data, causal inference methods, and academic policy writing.
Research Tasks: Literature review, data collection, data analysis, conducting interviews, etc. 
Skills that research assistant(s) may need: Required: Microsoft Excel proficiency; attention to detail; strong written English; ability to work independently.
Recommended: Coursework in economics/statistics/policy; exposure to Python; familiarity with housing policy or Section 8 vouchers; interest in graduate school or applied research careers.
Mentoring Philosophy
I value curiosity, active knowledge acquisition, and responsibility in the students I mentor. My approach treats research training as a mutual learning process: I bring project structure, standards, and my own experience, and students bring fresh questions, effort, and perspectives that often sharpen my own thinking. I hold high standards for rigor and accuracy, but alongside the recognition that early mistakes are how researchers learn to see problems clearly. I create space for mentees to try, be wrong, and try again. My role is to keep the intellectual space open and the standards clear; the growth belongs to the mentee.
