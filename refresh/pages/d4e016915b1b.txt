Research and Professional Interests:
One of the most exciting questions in biology is how new species arise. The goal of my research program is to understand both the proximate mechanisms and the ultimate evolutionary processes that generate biodiversity. My lab takes an integrative approach that combines behavioral ecology, evolutionary neuroscience, phylogenetics, population genetics, and genomics, using amphibians—especially chorus frogs ( Pseudacris )—as a model.
Current work in the lab has four main threads. First, we study speciation in action: how reinforcement in contact zones drives the evolution of mating signals, female preferences, and population-genetic structure, including cascade reinforcement beyond the contact zone. Second, we trace those behavioral changes into the nervous system, asking how auditory circuits and neurogenomic profiles diverge when species recognition is under selection. Third, we investigate the ecology and evolution of anuran biofluorescence, including whether fluorescent signals are tuned to the visual environment and whether they affect predation risk. Fourth, through the Center for Anchored Phylogenomics, we develop and apply targeted genomic methods to reconstruct the history of frogs and a wide range of other animals and plants, from continent-scale radiations to species delimitation in threatened amphibians.
