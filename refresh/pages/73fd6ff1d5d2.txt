Mousa Laboratory 
Biosketch
Dr. Mousa is a Professor in the Department of Biomedical Sciences at The Florida State University College of Medicine. The Mousa Laboratory studies antibody responses, both structurally and functionally, to natural infection and vaccination to develop new antibody therapeutics and epitope-focused vaccines. Dr. Mousa earned his Ph.D. under the direction of Dr. Steven D. Bruner in the Department of Chemistry at the University of Florida, where he studied the structure and function of multidrug resistance transporters used in natural product biosynthesis. For his postdoctoral training, Dr. Mousa moved to the Vanderbilt University Medical Center and joined the lab of Dr. James E. Crowe, Jr. in the Vanderbilt Vaccine Center where he was funded by the NIH T32 Vanderbilt Infection Pathogenesis and Epidemiology Research (VIPER) Training Program. During his postdoctoral training, Dr. Mousa studied the human immune response to respiratory syncytial virus infection. Dr. Mousa's research during graduate school and postdoctoral training resulted in several high-impact publications in Nature Microbiology , PNAS, and PLOS Pathogens . Following his postdoctoral training, Dr. Mousa moved to the Center for Vaccines and Immunology and the Department of Infectious Diseases at the University of Georgia in 2017 to start his independent research laboratory. In December 2023, Dr. Mousa moved his laboratory to The Florida State University College of Medicine and joined the Department of Biomedical Sciences. Dr. Mousa obtained an NIH HIV/AIDS Vaccine Scholars Program K01 grant in 2018, which facilitated his training in nonhuman primate models of infectious disease. Dr. Mousa has also been awarded multiple NIH R01 and R21 awards, as well as funding from the American Lung Association.

Research Focus
Antibody-mediated immunity is key to the majority of licensed vaccines and in protection following infection. At a high level, research in the Mousa Lab is focused on (A) Developing monoclonal antibody therapies for infectious pathogens, (B) Deciphering protein epitopes on pathogen surface proteins that elicit optimal protective antibodies, and (C) Designing and testing epitope-based vaccines designed from the findings from (A) and (B). We incorporate multiple techniques into our research program, including human immunology, flow cytometry, cryo-EM, and animal models of infectious diseases. For example, a major aspect of our laboratory is based on the isolation of antibodies from human subjects who have been previously infected or have been vaccinated against a particular pathogen. Once we isolate these antibodies, we examine their binding and functional properties, and also incorporate structural biology to identify the specific epitopes important for antibody recognition of antigens. In addition, we examine the antibodies as prophylactic and therapeutic drugs to prevent infection, while also using the information obtained from antibody characterization to design vaccines that could elicit antibodies to a particular epitope. Since starting my laboratory, we have focused on identifying antibodies and vaccines (new or improved) for human metapneumovirus, respiratory syncytial virus, parainfluenza viruses, influenza virus, and Streptococcus pneumoniae . We also have multiple other ongoing projects focused on additional pathogens.
